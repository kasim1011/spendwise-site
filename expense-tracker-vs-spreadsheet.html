Expense tracker vs spreadsheet: an honest comparison

A spreadsheet is the most honest budgeting tool ever made: free, transparent, and yours to shape. It also asks you to do the bookkeeping at a desk. This page is about where that trade stops working, and where it does not.

Updated: 20 October 2026  ·  Export: PDF and XLSX  ·  Import: none, by design

Where the spreadsheet genuinely wins

  • It costs nothing and installs nothing. You already have one, on every device, forever.
  • You own the formulas. Any view you can imagine — a savings rate, a rolling average, a per-payday split — is a column away.
  • Nothing is abstracted. You can see every cell, audit every total, and fix a mistake where it lives.
  • It doubles as the archive. One file holds years of history in a format that will outlive most apps.

If you are the kind of person who reconciles a sheet every Sunday with a coffee, an app is not going to improve your finances. It will only move your workflow.

Where it breaks

The failure is not the maths. It is the friction between the moment money leaves your account and the moment it becomes a row.

  • Entry at the till. Opening a file, finding the next empty row, typing a date, a category and an amount, then re-saving — on a phone — is a minute of work that nobody does at a checkout. The data arrives a week late, if at all.
  • No memory. A sheet does not remind you to log, and it does not know that rent is about to leave.
  • Categories by typo. One "Groceries" and one "Goceires" is how a category total becomes a lie without a formula error.
  • Version conflicts. Two people editing "Budget 2026 final v3.xlsx" is the household equivalent of a merge conflict, decided by whoever saved last.
  • Recurring costs live in your head. The rent reservation has to be modelled manually or the mid-month "budget left" figure is fiction.

Keep both: the export is the bridge

The reasonable answer for most people is not "replace the spreadsheet". It is "stop typing rows by hand, then export".

Spendwise exports a month or a year as XLSX — a Summary sheet with totals and the category breakdown, plus a monthly table — and as a PDF that mirrors the report on screen. Both are written into Download/Spendwise on Android, with the share sheet as a second route. Your spreadsheet becomes the analysis layer; the app becomes the capture layer.

What it will not do is import. There is no CSV import and no way to load an old sheet, so a migration means entering the past by hand or starting fresh from this month. That is a deliberate ceiling: parsing arbitrary spreadsheets well is a project of its own, and a bad import silently corrupts a ledger.

Which one to use

  • Stay with the sheet if you log weekly at a desk, want custom formulas, and already trust your process.
  • Use the app if expenses happen away from a desk, if you want reminders and a budget that knows rent is coming, or if receipts need to live with the expense.
  • Use both if you want fast capture and deep analysis: app on the phone, sheet for the rest, export at month end.

Frequently asked questions

Can I import my existing spreadsheet into Spendwise?

No. Spendwise has no CSV or spreadsheet import. Backup restore only accepts its own .spendwise file, so moving an old sheet means entering the history by hand, or starting clean from this month.

Can I export Spendwise data to a spreadsheet?

Yes. A month or a year exports to XLSX with a Summary sheet and a monthly table, alongside a PDF that mirrors the on-screen report. Both are written to Download/Spendwise on Android.

Does an app replace spreadsheet formulas?

No. The app answers budgeting questions directly and exports the numbers; it does not become a calculation surface. If you need custom formulas, keep the sheet and export into it.

Is a spreadsheet more private than an app?

It can be, if the file stays on your device and the suite does not sync it. Spendwise makes the same promise in a different shape: the ledger is a local database, there is no account, and no copy lives on a server — see what private means here.

Which is better for a household budget?

A spreadsheet if one person enters everything on a computer and wants custom reports. An app if expenses are logged as they happen on a phone, or if more than one person needs to see balances, since a sheet shared as a file invites version conflicts.
